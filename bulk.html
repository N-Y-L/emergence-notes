Chapter 1 · Entries 1–10

From particles to bulk behavior

I begin with a glass of water rather than an exotic material. Its molecules have positions, momenta, and interactions. The glass also has a temperature, a pressure, a viscosity, and a surface that rounds small drops. None of those macroscopic descriptions can be assigned to one isolated water molecule in the same operational sense. The task is to show how the descriptions become reliable, and which microscopic information they retain.

The first three entries develop equilibrium bookkeeping: temperature governs energy exchange, pressure records momentum transfer, and entropy counts the microscopic possibilities compatible with a macroscopic preparation. Diffusion, sound, and viscosity then ask how disturbances move or decay. Elasticity and surface tension introduce free-energy costs associated with deformation and interfaces. Screening shows why a force measured inside a material already includes the rearrangement of its surroundings.

Hydrodynamics gathers these ideas into a general strategy. I retain slow fields, use conservation to constrain their evolution, and put unresolved microscopic processes into measurable material coefficients. This is a particularly concrete form of emergence: a change in the variables and laws useful at a chosen scale. It does not require a sharp phase transition, and it does not mean that every microscopic detail becomes irrelevant. Each entry identifies the assumptions that make the reduction work.

01Temperature: Why two different thermometers agree

Established phenomenon · coarse graining · conservation

I put a gas thermometer and a small metal thermometer into the same insulated bath. Their atoms, energy spectra, and readouts differ, yet after equilibration they identify one temperature. The puzzle is why a single number can describe energy exchange between such unlike systems. Temperature is not the energy of an average atom: molecules may store energy in rotation, vibration, interactions, and other degrees of freedom.

I begin with two weakly coupled bodies that exchange energy while their combined energy stays fixed. Let ΩA(EA)\Omega_A(E_A)ΩA​(EA​) count the microscopic states compatible with the macroscopic constraints on body AAA, and define its entropy by SA=kBln⁡ΩAS_A=k_B\ln\Omega_ASA​=kB​lnΩA​, where kBk_BkB​ is Boltzmann's constant. The number of combined states is approximately the product ΩA(EA)ΩB(E−EA)\Omega_A(E_A)\Omega_B(E-E_A)ΩA​(EA​)ΩB​(E−EA​). At its maximum,

∂SA∂EA=∂SB∂EB,1T=(∂S∂E)V,N.\frac{\partial S_A}{\partial E_A}=\frac{\partial S_B}{\partial E_B},\qquad \frac1T=\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial E}\right)_{V,N}.∂EA​∂SA​​=∂EB​∂SB​​,T1​=(∂E∂S​)V,N​.

Here VVV is volume and NNN particle number; both are held fixed. The quantity equalized by energy exchange is the entropy slope, which defines reciprocal absolute temperature. Many microscopic energy distributions therefore share the same criterion for equilibrium. A thermometer works because its own measurable property can be calibrated against this common parameter.

For a small system coupled to a much larger bath, expanding the bath entropy to first order in the exchanged energy gives a state probability proportional to e−Ei/(kBT)e^{-E_i/(k_BT)}e−Ei​/(kB​T), where EiE_iEi​ is the small system's state energy. This is the Boltzmann distribution. It explains why the same temperature controls a gas's velocity distribution and a spin's level populations without assigning them the same total energy. Classical quadratic degrees of freedom each contribute mean energy kBT/2k_BT/2kB​T/2; quantum level spacing limits that particular rule.

The construction requires an equilibrium description and adequate time for energy exchange. A driven sample may have different distributions for electrons and atomic vibrations, or no useful single temperature. Even at equilibrium, strong coupling complicates the separation into body and thermometer. What emerges is a transferable parameter for a specified class of exchanges, supported by the overwhelming concentration of accessible states around equilibrium.

02Pressure: From molecular impacts to a smooth force

Established phenomenon · coarse graining · conservation

A piston feels a nearly steady push even though a dilute gas strikes it in separate, irregular impacts. I want to know how those impacts become pressure, a force per unit area that remains useful without tracking individual molecules. The key observable is momentum crossing a surface, averaged over an area and a time large compared with molecular scales.

Take NNN noninteracting particles of mass mmm in a rectangular box of volume VVV. A particle with velocity component vxv_xvx​ reverses its normal momentum on a reflecting wall, transferring 2m∣vx∣2m|v_x|2m∣vx​∣. Its return time to that wall is 2Lx/∣vx∣2L_x/|v_x|2Lx​/∣vx​∣, with LxL_xLx​ the box length. Dividing the average force by wall area and summing particles gives

p=NmV⟨vx2⟩=Nm3V⟨v2⟩.p=\frac{Nm}{V}\langle v_x^2\rangle=\frac{Nm}{3V}\langle v^2\rangle.p=VNm​⟨vx2​⟩=3VNm​⟨v2⟩.

The second equality uses an isotropic velocity distribution: no direction is preferred. With the equilibrium classical result m⟨v2⟩=3kBTm\langle v^2\rangle=3k_BTm⟨v2⟩=3kB​T, this becomes pV=NkBTpV=Nk_BTpV=NkB​T. Here kBk_BkB​ is Boltzmann's constant. Temperature TTT and number density N/VN/VN/V together determine the mean push. The wall records microscopic momentum transfer; it does not create the pressure.

I can imagine an internal plane through the gas and repeat the momentum accounting. Particles crossing it transport momentum between neighboring regions. In equilibrium this transport has equal normal components in every direction. Away from equilibrium, different components need not agree, and a stress tensor is needed: it records which component of force acts across a surface of each orientation. Interactions also carry momentum across the plane, so the ideal-gas formula cannot be applied unchanged to a dense liquid. The thermodynamic counterpart, p=−(∂F/∂V)T,Np=-(\partial F/\partial V)_{T,N}p=−(∂F/∂V)T,N​, follows from the reversible work of changing volume; FFF is Helmholtz free energy.

The smooth description is an averaging limit. A sufficiently small sensor resolves fluctuations, and a rapidly driven gas may retain directional velocity differences. Macroscopic pressure is therefore neither an extra microscopic force nor merely a name for collisions. It is a compact description of momentum transfer whose scalar form depends on local isotropy. Its success lets me predict piston motion without predicting the next collision.

03Entropy: Why mixing has a direction

Established phenomenon · coarse graining · constraints

I remove a partition that confines a gas to half a box. The particles obey reversible microscopic laws, yet the gas spreads and does not normally gather back into its original half. The interesting question is not whether a reversed trajectory exists. It does. The question is why almost every microscopic preparation compatible with the initial macroscopic conditions produces the same direction of change.

A macrostate specifies a limited set of observables, such as particle counts in spatial cells. A microstate specifies the detailed particle positions and momenta. Boltzmann entropy assigns SB=kBln⁡ΩS_B=k_B\ln\OmegaSB​=kB​lnΩ to a macrostate containing Ω\OmegaΩ compatible microstates; kBk_BkB​ is Boltzmann's constant. For an ideal gas, opening the other half roughly doubles each particle's accessible position volume. Thus the equilibrium entropy difference is

ΔSB=NkBln⁡2.\Delta S_B=Nk_B\ln 2.ΔSB​=NkB​ln2.

Here NNN is the particle number, and energy and total volume after expansion are fixed. At equilibrium, independent uniformly distributed positions put all particles in the original half with probability 2−N2^{-N}2−N. That probability measures the rarity of the old configuration; it is not a proof of a particular equilibration time.

I now have to keep two entropies distinct. For a classical probability density ρ\rhoρ on phase space, the fine-grained Gibbs entropy −kB∫ρln⁡ρ dΓ-k_B\int\rho\ln\rho\,d\Gamma−kB​∫ρlnρdΓ stays constant under Hamiltonian evolution, with a fixed reference measure understood. The evolving distribution stretches into detailed correlations. A coarse observation ignores those correlations and assigns growing weight to larger macrostates. For an isolated quantum system, the analogous full-state von Neumann entropy is likewise unchanged by unitary evolution, although the entropy of a subsystem may increase as it becomes entangled with its surroundings.

Entropy increase consequently needs both the chosen macroscopic description and an initially special preparation. Reversibility alone cannot select a time direction, and coarse-graining alone cannot guarantee that every trajectory relaxes. Integrable systems, which possess unusually many conserved quantities, and poorly mixing dynamics need additional care. In ordinary equilibrating matter, microscopic correlations become inaccessible to the selected measurements, while large-volume macrostates dominate what those measurements report. The emergent regularity is probabilistic irreversibility at the observational scale, with precisely stated exceptions rather than a violation of mechanics.

Something I can try asking. Which microscopic correlations would have to be controlled to reverse a gas expansion while keeping its macroscopic energy unchanged?

04Diffusion: How aimless steps make a predictable profile

Established phenomenon · coarse graining · conservation

A spot of dye spreads through still water even though no molecule consults the concentration gradient. I start with a simpler puzzle: a walker steps left or right by distance aaa every time interval τ\tauτ, with independent equal probabilities. Each trajectory looks erratic. Nevertheless, after nnn steps its mean displacement is zero and its mean squared displacement is na2na^2na2. Predictability appears in the distribution, not in an individual path.

Let P(x,t)P(x,t)P(x,t) be the probability density of position. The discrete update averages the two neighboring values. Expanding over distances much larger than aaa and times much longer than τ\tauτ gives

∂tP=D∂x2P,D=a22τ,⟨x2⟩=2Dt.\partial_tP=D\partial_x^2P,\qquad D=\frac{a^2}{2\tau},\qquad \langle x^2\rangle=2Dt.∂t​P=D∂x2​P,D=2τa2​,⟨x2⟩=2Dt.

The diffusion coefficient DDD has units of area per time. A sharply localized initial distribution becomes a Gaussian of width proportional to t\sqrt{t}t​. Doubling a diffusion distance therefore requires four times as long. The same equation can describe the concentration ccc of many independent particles because their probabilities add.

For material transport, conservation supplies ∂tc+∇ ⁣⋅j=0\partial_tc+\nabla\!\cdot\mathbf j=0∂t​c+∇⋅j=0, where j\mathbf jj is particle flux, the number crossing unit area per unit time. A local linear response j=−D∇c\mathbf j=-D\nabla cj=−D∇c then yields the diffusion equation. This is Fick's law. Its minus sign does not give molecules a directional intention: more particles start on the concentrated side, so unbiased crossings leave a net imbalance. In interacting mixtures, chemical potential, the free-energy cost of adding particles, is the more general driving variable. A concentration gradient alone need not predict the flux.

The random-walk derivation makes its limits visible. Persistent velocities give ballistic motion at short times, with mean squared displacement proportional to t2t^2t2. Long waiting times, spatial obstacles, or long-lived correlations can prevent ordinary diffusion. Flow adds transport by the moving solvent, and reactions alter particle conservation. I therefore look for a time window in which displacement memory has decayed and an approximately constant DDD describes the measured spreading. In that window, the collective profile forgets most details of the microscopic steps while retaining their accumulated variance.

Something I can try asking. How would you distinguish slow ordinary diffusion from a process whose mean squared displacement has a genuinely different time dependence?

05Sound: A disturbance moves while matter stays nearby

Established phenomenon · collective modes · conservation

A spoken word crosses a room much faster than the air from the speaker's mouth. What travels is a correlated pattern of compression and velocity. Individual air molecules undergo fast disordered motion, while the small average displacement associated with the sound remains local. I want an equation for that pattern without following any particular molecule.

Write the mass density as ρ0+δρ\rho_0+\delta\rhoρ0​+δρ and the pressure as p0+δpp_0+\delta pp0​+δp, where the subscript denotes a uniform equilibrium state. Let v\mathbf vv be the small local mean velocity. To first order, mass conservation and momentum balance give

∂tδρ+ρ0∇ ⁣⋅v=0,ρ0∂tv=−∇δp.\partial_t\delta\rho+\rho_0\nabla\!\cdot\mathbf v=0,\qquad \rho_0\partial_t\mathbf v=-\nabla\delta p.∂t​δρ+ρ0​∇⋅v=0,ρ0​∂t​v=−∇δp.

A compression changes pressure through the material's equation of state. For an approximately adiabatic sound disturbance, which exchanges negligible heat between neighboring compressed and expanded regions during a cycle, δp=cs2δρ\delta p=c_s^2\delta\rhoδp=cs2​δρ, with cs2=(∂p/∂ρ)sc_s^2=(\partial p/\partial\rho)_{s}cs2​=(∂p/∂ρ)s​ and sss the entropy per unit mass. Combining the equations produces ∂t2δρ=cs2∇2δρ\partial_t^2\delta\rho=c_s^2\nabla^2\delta\rho∂t2​δρ=cs2​∇2δρ. A pressure excess accelerates adjacent fluid; its motion builds the next compression.

For an ideal gas, cs2=γp0/ρ0c_s^2=\gamma p_0/\rho_0cs2​=γp0​/ρ0​, where γ=Cp/CV\gamma=C_p/C_Vγ=Cp​/CV​ is the ratio of heat capacities at constant pressure and volume. This clarifies why density alone cannot determine the speed: restoring stiffness matters too. A dense but hard-to-compress material may carry sound rapidly. The mode is collective because the phases of many local density and velocity changes fit one propagating solution. The wavelength is a property of their coordinated pattern, rather than a molecule's flight length.

The wave equation assumes small amplitudes and local equilibration on times shorter than the oscillation period. Viscosity and heat conduction attenuate real sound; strong compression makes the propagation speed amplitude dependent and can form a shock. If wavelengths approach the molecular mean free path, the average flight distance between collisions, the hydrodynamic derivation loses its basis. The successful wave description therefore selects both an observable and a range of scales. Beyond that range, I must restore the microscopic relaxation processes that were hidden inside the equation of state.

Something I can try asking. Which measurement would distinguish a change in sound speed caused by compressibility from one caused by density?

06Viscosity: Friction without rubbing solid surfaces

Established phenomenon · coarse graining · conservation

I slide one plate over another with a fluid between them. Even when the plates never touch, a force opposes the motion. The fluid layers transfer momentum: a faster layer loses some of its directed motion to slower neighbors. Viscosity is the coefficient relating this momentum transfer to the rate at which the fluid is sheared, meaning that adjacent layers move at different velocities.

For a Newtonian fluid in a steady, simple shear flow, the constitutive relation is

σxy=η ∂yvx.\sigma_{xy}=\eta\,\partial_yv_x.σxy​=η∂y​vx​.

Here vxv_xvx​ is the mean velocity along the plates, yyy is distance across the gap, σxy\sigma_{xy}σxy​ is the tangential stress acting across a surface normal to yyy, and η\etaη is dynamic shear viscosity, measured in pascal seconds. For plate area AAA, gap hhh, and relative speed UUU, negligible slip at the surfaces gives a linear velocity profile and a required force magnitude F=ηAU/hF=\eta AU/hF=ηAU/h. “Newtonian” names this linear stress–strain-rate relation; it does not simply mean that the molecules obey Newton's laws.

The microscopic route differs among materials. In a dilute gas, molecules carry their momentum between collisions, producing the estimate η∼ρvthℓ\eta\sim\rho v_{\rm th}\ellη∼ρvth​ℓ, where ρ\rhoρ is mass density, vthv_{\rm th}vth​ a thermal speed, and ℓ\ellℓ a mean free path. This explains a useful surprise: increasing density can shorten the mean free path enough to compensate the extra momentum carriers. In a dense liquid, intermolecular forces and rearrangements dominate, so that gas estimate is not a general viscosity formula. Both routes yield the same leading continuum relation when stresses relax rapidly enough.

The work done by the plates becomes internal energy. In this simple shear, the dissipation rate per volume is η(∂yvx)2\eta(\partial_yv_x)^2η(∂y​vx​)2, which is nonnegative for a passive fluid. The associated momentum diffusivity is ν=η/ρ\nu=\eta/\rhoν=η/ρ. Polymer solutions and suspensions can remember deformation or change viscosity with shear rate; at molecular distances even local stress needs careful definition. The emerging coefficient summarizes otherwise inaccessible relaxation processes, but its dependence on temperature, frequency, and forcing still carries information about those processes.

07Elasticity: How a solid remembers its shape

Established phenomenon · constraints · collective modes

A metal rod returns nearly to its old length after a small pull. Its atoms were never fixed points; they vibrated before, during, and after the experiment. The shape memory instead comes from a stable collective arrangement and the free-energy cost of deforming it. I need to describe relative displacements, because moving the entire rod without changing its shape should cost no internal elastic energy.

Let u(r)\mathbf u(\mathbf r)u(r) be the displacement of material initially at position r\mathbf rr. For small deformations, the strain tensor is ϵij=(∂iuj+∂jui)/2\epsilon_{ij}=(\partial_i u_j+\partial_j u_i)/2ϵij​=(∂i​uj​+∂j​ui​)/2. It removes a small rigid rotation and measures changes of lengths and angles. Expanding free energy about an unstressed stable configuration gives

F−F0=12∫Cijklϵijϵkl dV,σij=Cijklϵkl.F-F_0=\frac12\int C_{ijkl}\epsilon_{ij}\epsilon_{kl}\,dV,\qquad \sigma_{ij}=C_{ijkl}\epsilon_{kl}.F−F0​=21​∫Cijkl​ϵij​ϵkl​dV,σij​=Cijkl​ϵkl​.

Repeated indices are summed. The coefficients CijklC_{ijkl}Cijkl​ are elastic stiffnesses; σij\sigma_{ij}σij​ is stress, the iii-directed force per area across a surface normal to direction jjj. The linear stress law follows from the quadratic free-energy term, rather than requiring every atomic bond to be an exactly linear spring.

For an isotropic solid, whose response is independent of direction, two independent moduli suffice: one penalizes volume change, the other shear. In a crystal, the lattice symmetry generally permits more independent stiffnesses. Atomic interactions supply restoring forces, but thermal fluctuations and internal relaxation also affect the measured coefficients. Rubber gives a particularly useful warning against a purely bond-energy picture: stretching a network reduces the number of accessible polymer configurations, so entropy can provide much of the restoring force.

Static shear rigidity distinguishes a solid on the measurement time scale from an ordinary fluid, which continually rearranges under shear. Glasses can be rigid without crystalline periodicity, and viscoelastic materials combine reversible deformation with time-dependent flow. At large strains, defects move, bonds break, or rearrangements produce permanent deformation, outside the small-strain expansion. The emergent object is thus a displacement field with a reproducible restoring law, sustained by constraints that persist long enough for the experiment. Asking how long they persist is as important as asking how stiff each microscopic bond is.

Something I can try asking. If a material returns after a fast pull but flows under a slow load, which time scale must an elastic description include?

08Surface tension: Why a drop behaves as if it has a skin

Established phenomenon · coarse graining · constraints

A small isolated drop rounds itself even though its surface contains the same molecules as its interior. I do not need to imagine a separate elastic wrapper. Molecules near the interface have a different neighborhood, and creating more interface changes the free energy. Surface tension γ\gammaγ measures that reversible free-energy cost per unit area under specified thermodynamic conditions.

For a clean interface with approximately constant γ\gammaγ, the surface contribution is Fs=γAF_s=\gamma AFs​=γA, where AAA is area. Consider a spherical drop of radius RRR. Increasing its radius changes area and volume by dA=8πR dRdA=8\pi R\,dRdA=8πRdR and dV=4πR2 dRdV=4\pi R^2\,dRdV=4πR2dR. Balancing the surface cost against pressure work gives

Δp dV=γ dA,Δp=2γR.\Delta p\,dV=\gamma\,dA,\qquad \Delta p=\frac{2\gamma}{R}.ΔpdV=γdA,Δp=R2γ​.

The pressure inside exceeds that outside by Δp\Delta pΔp. This is the spherical Young–Laplace relation for one interface; a thin soap bubble has two interfaces and a different factor. More generally, two principal curvatures determine the pressure jump. A smaller drop therefore needs a larger pressure difference to support its more strongly curved surface.

Why is a sphere selected? At fixed volume it minimizes surface area, provided other energies and constraints can be neglected. Gravity competes with surface tension over the capillary length ℓc=γ/(Δρg)\ell_c=\sqrt{\gamma/(\Delta\rho g)}ℓc​=γ/(Δρg)​, where Δρ\Delta\rhoΔρ is the density difference between phases and ggg gravitational acceleration. Drops much smaller than this length are shaped mainly by surface energy; larger drops flatten. On a solid substrate, the free energies of three interfaces also select a contact angle, so a free sphere is no longer the appropriate comparison.

The surface coefficient includes energetic and entropic contributions. “Missing neighbors” is a useful molecular starting picture, but does not calculate the full tension or explain every interface. Dissolved molecules that accumulate at the surface can change γ\gammaγ; gradients of tension then drive fluid motion. At radii comparable with the interfacial thickness, treating the boundary as an infinitely thin surface with a constant coefficient becomes inaccurate. Surface tension is an effective property of an interfacial region, made useful by a separation between molecular thickness and macroscopic curvature.

Something I can try asking. At what drop size should gravity noticeably defeat rounding, and which independently measurable quantities set that size?

09Screening: How many charges hide one charge

Established phenomenon · feedback · collective modes

A point charge in vacuum produces a potential proportional to 1/r1/r1/r. Put it in a dilute electrolyte, and the potential can decay much faster. The microscopic electric force has not changed. Mobile ions redistribute: opposite charges accumulate nearby, like charges are depleted, and their combined field weakens the original disturbance. Screening is this collective reduction of a perturbing field.

I use a symmetric electrolyte with charges +q+q+q and −q-q−q, each at bulk number density n0n_0n0​, in a solvent represented by permittivity ε\varepsilonε. At equilibrium temperature TTT, with Boltzmann's constant kBk_BkB​, dilute-ion Boltzmann distributions give n±=n0exp⁡(∓qϕ/kBT)n_\pm=n_0\exp(\mp q\phi/k_BT)n±​=n0​exp(∓qϕ/kB​T) for electric potential ϕ\phiϕ. When ∣qϕ∣≪kBT|q\phi|\ll k_BT∣qϕ∣≪kB​T, their net charge density is q(n+−n−)≃−2n0q2ϕ/(kBT)q(n_+-n_-)\simeq-2n_0q^2\phi/(k_BT)q(n+​−n−​)≃−2n0​q2ϕ/(kB​T). Poisson's equation then becomes, outside the inserted charge,

∇2ϕ=ϕλD2,λD=εkBT2n0q2.\nabla^2\phi=\frac{\phi}{\lambda_D^2},\qquad \lambda_D=\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon k_BT}{2n_0q^2}}.∇2ϕ=λD2​ϕ​,λD​=2n0​q2εkB​T​​.

The Debye length λD\lambda_DλD​ is the screening distance. For a weak point perturbation of charge QQQ, the three-dimensional solution is ϕ(r)=Qe−r/λD/(4πεr)\phi(r)=Qe^{-r/\lambda_D}/(4\pi\varepsilon r)ϕ(r)=Qe−r/λD​/(4πεr). Thermal spreading competes with electrostatic rearrangement: increasing temperature broadens the cloud, while increasing mobile-ion density shortens it.

The derivation is self-consistent. I first let the potential change the ion distribution, then include the field generated by that changed distribution. Using the bare charge's field throughout would miss the feedback. In a sufficiently large neutral system the screening cloud compensates the inserted charge, with boundaries and total-charge constraints determining where any remaining compensation resides. The response concerns the combined source and medium; it does not say that an individual ion loses its charge.

Linearization fails close to a strongly charged object. Finite ion sizes, strong correlations, and concentrated solutions can also invalidate the dilute mean-field description. Metals screen too, but their electron occupations obey quantum statistics, producing a different response law and sometimes oscillatory density patterns. I therefore treat the exponential form as a derived result for a specific regime, not a universal definition of screening. The broader emergent idea is that an interaction measured inside matter already contains the medium's organized response.

Something I can try asking. What change in the measured screening profile would show that a dilute, linear-response description has stopped being adequate?

10Hydrodynamics: Why a few fields can replace an enormous state

Established phenomenon · conservation · coarse graining

A water flow contains an astronomical number of molecular coordinates, yet its large-scale motion can often be predicted from density, velocity, and temperature. I want to know why these variables survive when almost everything microscopic is discarded. The answer begins with conservation: a locally conserved quantity can leave a region only by crossing its boundary, so a long-wavelength imbalance generally relaxes slowly.

For an ordinary one-component fluid, mass, momentum, and energy provide the basic slow fields. Local equilibrium means that a small region has relaxed internally while remaining small compared with the scale over which the macroscopic fields vary. Its pressure can then be supplied by an equilibrium equation of state. Conservation of mass reads ∂tρ+∇ ⁣⋅(ρv)=0\partial_t\rho+\nabla\!\cdot(\rho\mathbf v)=0∂t​ρ+∇⋅(ρv)=0, with mass density ρ\rhoρ and local mean velocity v\mathbf vv.

For an incompressible Newtonian fluid with constant ρ\rhoρ and shear viscosity η\etaη, momentum balance becomes

ρ[∂tv+(v ⁣⋅∇)v]=−∇p+η∇2v+f,∇ ⁣⋅v=0.\rho\big[\partial_t\mathbf v+(\mathbf v\!\cdot\nabla)\mathbf v\big]=-\nabla p+\eta\nabla^2\mathbf v+\mathbf f,\qquad \nabla\!\cdot\mathbf v=0.ρ[∂t​v+(v⋅∇)v]=−∇p+η∇2v+f,∇⋅v=0.

Here ppp is pressure and f\mathbf ff an external force per volume. The term (v ⁣⋅∇)v(\mathbf v\!\cdot\nabla)\mathbf v(v⋅∇)v accounts for acceleration caused by moving into a region with a different velocity, even in a steady flow. These are the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations. Conservation determines the balance structure; a constitutive assumption, the linear relation between viscous stress and velocity gradients, supplies the material closure. Conservation alone does not determine viscosity.

Hydrodynamics can therefore apply to chemically different liquids once their transport coefficients and boundary conditions are specified. The required separation of scales can fail near rarefied-gas boundaries, very rapid driving, or molecular confinement. Additional slow variables must be retained in mixtures, liquid crystals, or superfluids. Large-scale complexity is still possible: the nonlinear equations support vortices and turbulent motion. Emergence here means a controlled change of description, not an assurance that the resulting equations are easy to solve. I have replaced many fast variables with a small set of fields whose evolution is constrained by what the microscopic dynamics cannot quickly erase.

Something I can try asking. Which extra field would you need if a fluid contained a slowly relaxing molecular orientation in addition to conserved mass, momentum, and energy?
