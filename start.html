What we mean by emergence

We put one water molecule on the table—at least in our imagination—and ask for its viscosity. The question has arrived at the wrong scale. We need many molecules, their interactions, a way to impose flow, and a timescale on which stress can relax. Only then does a viscosity become a useful, measurable property.

Now we put a hundred people in a room. A market, a language, or a wise decision does not automatically appear. The interactions and the setting matter. Emergence, as we use the word here, is the appearance of a useful larger-scale property, pattern, or rule from the organization and interaction of smaller-scale constituents. The definition is deliberately operational: we want to know what we can predict or explain with the new description.

In these notes we catalog one hundred interesting examples, chosen to compare mechanisms and suggest questions to explore. They range from statistical physics and living systems to social patterns, computation, and a few open research questions. This is a selection, not an exhaustive list. The entries specify what emerges and what supports the explanation; they do not all use one mathematical definition of emergence.

Four questions for each example

  1. What are the constituents and the rules? A model must say what can change, how components interact, and what the surroundings supply or remove.
  2. What appears at the larger scale? we need an observable: a pressure, an ordered fraction, a wave speed, a correlation, a stable convention, or a reproducible computational behavior.
  3. Why does that description work? Averaging, feedback, a broken symmetry, a constraint, instability, selection, and learning are different answers. Naming emergence does not replace one.
  4. When does it stop working? Finite size, short observation times, changed boundaries, neglected variables, and altered interactions may destroy the apparent law.

A microstate specifies the detailed variables retained by our model. A macrostate specifies a smaller set of collective variables. These words are relative: a neuron, or nerve cell, is macroscopic compared with its molecules and microscopic compared with a brain model. Coarse-graining is a procedure that discards some detail, for example by averaging density over a small volume. It succeeds when, on the scales we care about, the retained variables have a predictive description that does not require tracking every discarded detail. Averaging is easy; obtaining a predictive law after averaging is the interesting part.

Different kinds of emergence

A pressure is a bulk variable. A phonon is one quantum of a coordinated lattice vibration. Magnetization is an order parameter, a quantity used to characterize ordering. A traffic jam is a dynamical pattern. Cooperation is a relation among behaviors and consequences in a specified evolutionary or social setting. A cellular automaton is a grid of cells updated by specified local rules; a theorem about it is a result about a mathematical model. These examples call for different kinds of explanation and evidence.

We use three labels throughout the notes. Established phenomenon means that the central behavior is observed and well supported; it does not certify every proposed explanation or every material example. Model result means that the displayed conclusion belongs to a specified mathematical or computational construction; additional evidence is needed to apply it to a real population or device. Open interpretation means that a proposed account, its generality, or the relevant notion of emergence is unresolved. An entry can contain established facts inside an open interpretation, and the text says which is which.

The existence of an effective description does not require new fundamental forces. Nor must an emergent behavior be surprising to everyone: diffusion can be derived in elementary models and remains an important larger-scale law. Philosophical discussions sometimes reserve strong emergence for properties claimed not to be reducible to or derivable from underlying physical facts even in principle. We do not assume that claim here. The notes concern explanations we can formulate, test, and delimit. Anderson's “More Is Different” (1972) is a classic argument for the importance of new organizing principles at different scales; it is not a license to stop explaining.

Here covariance measures how two variables fluctuate together. Independence removes the off-diagonal terms, giving Var⁡(xˉ)=σ2/N\operatorname{Var}(\bar x)=\sigma^2/NVar(xˉ)=σ2/N. This explains why a noisy collection can have a reliable average. It also exposes the loophole: correlated constituents can fluctuate together, so increasing their number need not provide the same improvement. Critical fluctuations, synchronized oscillators, and socially correlated estimates exploit that loophole in different ways.

We cannot infer the mechanism merely from a smooth average or a dramatic jump. A sharp threshold can come from a decision rule applied to smooth data. A power law—a relation of the form y∝xay\propto x^ay∝xa over a stated range—can arise by several mechanisms. A computer picture that resembles a cell or a city is evidence that the rules can make that picture, not yet evidence that they explain the cell or city. The strongest examples combine a model, discriminating predictions, and observations or experiments that could have contradicted them.

How to read these notes

For physics we assume graduate quantum mechanics and mathematics, some condensed matter, and the usual undergraduate core. For biology, neuroscience, economics, and computing we begin with the specialized vocabulary rather than treating familiar-sounding words as explanations. Each entry is independently approachable; the glossary is a reminder, not a substitute for local definitions. The numbered chapters provide a continuous route, while the reading paths follow mechanisms across disciplines.

The six interactive models are small enough to inspect. Their default figures and the equations are already saved in the pages, so reading does not depend on JavaScript. Controls recompute stated models. A simulation is always finite, an approximation is labeled, and exported records include the parameters and random seed where relevant. The question below each entry is an invitation to change a mechanism or design a test, rather than a request to memorize a label.

For related physics, see the anyon notes and transport notes. Bartosz Ciechanowski's interactive Sound also shows how molecular motion produces a macroscopic wave.
