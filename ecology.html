Ecology and evolution: collectives with histories

Ecology studies organisms' interactions with one another and their environment. Evolution concerns changes in inherited characteristics across generations. The two are coupled: organisms alter resources and competitors, while these conditions affect which organisms reproduce. Collective behavior therefore depends on both present interactions and the history that produced them.

We begin with selection, evolutionary innovation, cooperation, and task allocation. Flocking then provides an example of organization on a much shorter time scale, as individuals coordinate their motion without evolving during the event. The remaining entries consider communities: the stability of interaction networks, recovery from disturbance, succession, infection spread, and the exchange of nutrients between microbes. Simple models identify mechanisms and measurable quantities. Experiments determine which of those mechanisms matter in a particular population or environment.

51Natural selection: Heritable differences change a population

Natural selection occurs when heritable differences affect reproductive success. Heritable means that descendants tend to retain the relevant differences. If one bacterial variant leaves more descendants in a nutrient solution, its share of the population can increase even though no individual improves during its lifetime. Fitness measures reproductive contribution under specified conditions; it depends on the environment and on the other organisms present.

For two faithfully inherited variants, let ppp be the frequency of type AAA and let wA,wBw_A,w_BwA​,wB​ be their expected numbers of descendants. In a deterministic generation model, p′=pwA/[pwA+(1−p)wB]p'=p w_A/[p w_A+(1-p)w_B]p′=pwA​/[pwA​+(1−p)wB​]. Thus AAA increases when wA>wBw_A>w_BwA​>wB​. This is an accounting relation once reproductive rates are specified. Resource use, survival, and interactions explain those rates and why their ordering can change.

In Lenski and Travisano's long-term experiment, replicate bacterial populations adapted to the same laboratory conditions over ten thousand generations while diverging from one another. Repeated exposure to the same conditions produced a common direction of adaptation without fixing a unique genetic route. Mutations—changes in hereditary sequence—supply new variation. Genetic drift adds random changes in variant frequencies through finite sampling of reproduction and survival.

Adaptation is the improved fit to particular conditions that selection can produce. It need not increase every measure of performance: using one resource more efficiently may reduce performance on another, and a variant's advantage may disappear as it becomes common. Nor is every frequent trait adaptive. A neutral difference can spread because it is inherited together with a beneficial one. The population-level outcome is a changing distribution of traits, shaped jointly by selective differences, chance, and the available hereditary variation.

References
  • Lenski & Travisano (1994), Dynamics of adaptation and diversification: a 10,000-generation experiment with bacterial populations. Measures adaptation relative to an ancestor and divergence among replicate E. coli populations over ten thousand generations.

52Evolutionary innovation: New capabilities depend on evolutionary history

An evolutionary innovation is a capability or organization absent from a lineage's ancestors. It can arise by changing when an existing component is used or by combining components differently. The effect of a new mutation therefore depends on the system in which it occurs, making the route to a capability part of its explanation.

The long-term Escherichia coli experiment offers a concrete case. Its medium contained citrate, a carbon-containing molecule that the ancestral bacteria could not use as a carbon source under the experiment's oxygenated conditions. One population evolved that ability. Blount, Borland, and Lenski restarted evolution from stored samples and found that later genetic backgrounds were more likely to produce citrate-using descendants. Molecular work then identified changes that enabled and refined the new metabolism, the chemical transformations supporting growth and maintenance.

We can represent the dependence using two genetic changes, aaa and bbb. If the effect of bbb differs according to whether aaa is present, the changes exhibit epistasis. Their effects cannot be treated as independent contributions. A useful combination may become accessible after an earlier change that originally spread for another reason. Equal current growth rates therefore need not imply equal access to future capabilities.

Think of hereditary sequences as vertices of a graph, with edges joining sequences accessible by one mutational step. Reproduction and loss determine which routes are occupied, while the environment changes the advantage of moving along them. Physical chemistry constrains the possible endpoints, but intermediate states constrain which endpoints a lineage can reach. Replaying evolution from preserved ancestors makes this historical dependence experimentally testable: it compares the distribution of subsequent outcomes from different starting backgrounds.

References
  • Blount, Borland & Lenski (2008), Historical contingency and the evolution of a key innovation in an experimental population of Escherichia coli. Uses replay experiments from preserved bacterial populations to test how prior evolution changes access to citrate use.
  • Quandt et al. (2015), Fine-tuning citrate synthase flux potentiates and refines metabolic innovation in the Lenski evolution experiment. Identifies changes in citrate synthase activity that contributed to the preparation for, and refinement of, citrate-use metabolism.

53Cooperation: A shared resource can sustain its producers

A cell may release an enzyme that makes food available to its neighbors. If producing the enzyme is costly, a nonproducer can benefit without contributing. Cooperation in evolutionary biology concerns effects that benefit another organism, assessed through reproduction; costly cooperation poses the additional problem of explaining why contributors persist in competition with noncontributors.

Yeast growing on sucrose provide a well-studied case. The enzyme invertase splits sucrose near the cell surface, and much of the resulting food diffuses away. In experiments by Gore and colleagues, producing cells retained a small direct benefit. Combined with a nonlinear relationship between available food and growth, this allowed producers and nonproducers each to increase when rare under suitable conditions. Neither type eliminated the other.

Let ppp be the producer fraction and fP(p),fN(p)f_P(p),f_N(p)fP​(p),fN​(p) their respective per-capita growth rates. Then p˙=p(1−p)[fP(p)−fN(p)]\dot p=p(1-p)[f_P(p)-f_N(p)]p˙​=p(1−p)[fP​(p)−fN​(p)]. If producers do better when rare but worse when common, a crossing of the two rates from positive to negative difference gives a stable mixed population. The maintained producer fraction follows from the dependence of growth on composition.

Spatial arrangement changes this dependence by determining who receives the released material. Limited diffusion can keep benefits near producers, while mixing spreads them more widely. Relatedness and repeated interactions can also alter how benefits return to contributors, but each requires a specified biological mechanism. Population productivity and resistance to invasion are separate outcomes: the mixture with the highest total yield need not be evolutionarily stable. Measuring both individual growth and shared-resource flows explains which cooperative arrangements can persist.

References
  • Gore, Youk & van Oudenaarden (2009), Snowdrift game dynamics and facultative cheating in yeast. Shows frequency-dependent coexistence of invertase producers and nonproducers, linked to nonlinear growth benefits and partial retention of released food.
  • Ohtsuki et al. (2006), A simple rule for the evolution of cooperation on graphs and social networks. Derives cooperation criteria for specified interaction graphs, selection strengths, and population update rules.

54Division of labor: Task specialization among similar individuals

Division of labor occurs when different individuals consistently perform different activities. Fixed differences in anatomy can create specialists, but similar individuals can also specialize through their interactions and experience. The relevant observation is persistence across time: a single snapshot of workers doing different things establishes only that several tasks are underway.

Consider an illustrative colony with food collection and care of young. Unfinished work increases a task signal, and a worker begins the task when that signal exceeds its response threshold. Completed work reduces the signal, limiting further recruitment. If performing a task also lowers that worker's threshold for it, small initial differences can grow into persistent specialization. Local responses to demand then allocate effort without requiring any individual to monitor the whole colony.

Ulrich and colleagues tested groups of clonal raider ants whose workers were closely matched in age and genetically alike. Larger groups developed stronger and more persistent differences in activity, even at small group sizes. Colony conditions became more stable and reproductive performance improved. Their model attributed much of the increased stability to group size itself and a smaller contribution to specialization. Thus the experiment and model distinguish the coexistence of two changes from the claim that one fully explains the other.

The strength of reinforcement creates a tradeoff. Persistent roles can reduce task switching, but workers must sometimes replace absent specialists or respond to new demand. Removing workers from a task and measuring how the remaining group reallocates effort tests this flexibility. The collective property is the distribution and persistence of task participation, together with its response to changing workload.

References
  • Ulrich et al. (2018), Fitness benefits and emergent division of labour at the onset of group living. Tracks specialization in clonal ant groups and uses a model to separate effects of group size and division of labor on colony stability.

55Collective motion: Local interactions coordinate a moving group

A flock can turn coherently even though each bird observes only part of the group. To isolate the coordination problem, we can model individuals as self-propelled particles that continuously use energy to move. Interactions align their headings, regulate separation, or avoid collisions. A common direction then becomes a property of the coupled trajectories.

In the Vicsek alignment model, each particle adopts the direction of the vector sum of its neighbors' headings, adds a random angular perturbation, and moves forward. This circular average avoids treating headings just below and above 2π2\pi2π as opposites. Polarization, P=∣∑ivi∣/∑i∣vi∣P=|\sum_i\mathbf v_i|/\sum_i|\mathbf v_i|P=∣∑i​vi​∣/∑i​∣vi​∣, measures directional order. Aligned velocities give P≃1P\simeq1P≃1; random headings in a large group largely cancel. Varying noise, density, and interactions changes whether ordered motion persists.

Which individuals count as neighbors is experimentally consequential. Ballerini and colleagues reconstructed starling flocks in three dimensions and found spatial structure consistent with an interaction range set by neighbor rank, rather than a single fixed physical distance. Such a rule can preserve connections as a flock expands or compresses. The result is evidence about the observed flocks, while other species and circumstances may involve different interaction rules.

Large polarization alone cannot distinguish social coordination from independent responses to wind, light, or another common cue. The propagation of a turn, including delays and changing neighbor relationships, supplies additional evidence. This connects the visible group pattern to the information available locally. Directional order, rapid collective turning, and resistance to fragmentation are measurable outcomes whose dependence on the interaction rule can be tested separately.

References
  • Ballerini et al. (2008), Interaction ruling animal collective behavior depends on topological rather than metric distance: Evidence from a field study. Infers a neighbor-rank interaction range from three-dimensional starling flock structure and compares cohesion under alternative model rules.
  • Vicsek et al. (1995), Novel type of phase transition in a system of self-driven particles. Demonstrates collective directional order in a minimal model of moving particles with local alignment and angular noise.

56Food webs: Interaction structure controls community stability

A food web records which organisms consume which others. Extra species and links can provide alternative resource pathways, but they also introduce routes through which disturbances spread. Their effect on stability depends on interaction strengths and organization, rather than on the number of species alone.

This is a statement about an ensemble of linearized models. A coexistence equilibrium must also be feasible, with positive abundances, and a locally stable state may fail after a large disturbance. Real communities are assembled through colonization, competition, and evolution, which impose structure absent from independent random entries. The random model is valuable because it identifies what needs explanation: correlations and constraints that alter collective growth and decay modes.

57Ecosystem resilience: Recovery rates and the size of a disturbance

Two ecosystems can have similar present conditions but respond very differently to the same disturbance. An ecosystem includes a community of organisms and its physical and chemical environment. Resilience can mean rapid recovery after a small disturbance, or the ability to withstand a large disturbance without switching to a different persistent regime. These properties need not change together.

For local recovery, write δx˙=−rδx\dot{\delta x}=-r\delta xδx˙=−rδx with r>0r>0r>0. The return time is 1/r1/r1/r. Near a bifurcation at which this recovery rate vanishes, disturbances decay increasingly slowly. If stationary white noise drives the linear system at fixed strength, its variance and temporal correlations increase as well. Changing noise strength or external forcing can produce similar statistics, so fluctuations alone do not identify the cause.

Experiments can directly test the disturbance response. Veraart and colleagues increased light levels toward a collapse threshold in cyanobacteria, bacteria that obtain energy through photosynthesis. Recovery from small disturbances slowed as the threshold approached. In a whole-lake experiment, Carpenter and colleagues observed warning statistics during food-web reorganization induced by adding top predators, using a reference lake for comparison.

Resistance to larger disturbances concerns the geometry beyond the linear neighborhood. If two stable regimes have different basins of attraction—the sets of states that return to each—a disturbance can carry the system across their boundary. A state may recover quickly from tiny displacements while lying close to that boundary. Measuring both recovery rates and the disturbances needed to trigger persistent change separates these meanings of resilience. It also explains why a sudden external shock can cause a transition without a preceding trend of slower recovery.

References
  • Carpenter et al. (2011), Early warnings of regime shifts: a whole-ecosystem experiment. Reports warning statistics during a manipulated lake food-web transition, with a reference lake for comparison.
  • Veraart et al. (2012), Recovery rates reflect distance to a tipping point in a living system. Tests recovery rates and temporal correlations in cyanobacterial populations approaching a light-induced collapse threshold.

58Ecological succession: Community change depends on earlier occupants

Ecological succession is change in the species composition of a community through time, often after a disturbance. A community consists of the populations of different species living together. Early arrivals can alter light, nutrients, space, and other conditions, changing which organisms can subsequently establish.

Connell and Slatyer distinguished three mechanisms. In facilitation, early occupants improve conditions for later species. In inhibition, they prevent or delay establishment by occupying space or consuming resources. In tolerance, later species can establish without this assistance and eventually persist because they tolerate conditions that exclude earlier occupants. Different mechanisms can operate at different stages or between different species pairs.

A simple approximation exposes the causal question. Let EEE be the abundance of an early colonist and let a rare later species have per-capita growth gL=rL+aEg_L=r_L+aEgL​=rL​+aE. Positive aaa represents facilitation, negative aaa inhibition, and a≃0a\simeq0a≃0 little direct effect in this approximation. The fact that one species appears first does not determine aaa: both species may respond independently to changing soil or weather. Experiments that add or remove early occupants, while accounting for the disturbance caused by the intervention, can distinguish these possibilities.

Arrival also depends on dispersal, the movement of organisms or reproductive material between places. Suitable conditions do not guarantee that a species has reached the site. Different arrival orders can therefore produce different communities under otherwise similar conditions. Repeated disturbance may prevent a persistent endpoint, and a changing environment can alter which endpoints are possible. Succession is a trajectory of interacting populations and their surroundings; explaining it requires the effects of earlier occupants as well as the conditions observed today.

References
  • Connell & Slatyer (1977), Mechanisms of Succession in Natural Communities and Their Role in Community Stability and Organization. Formulates facilitation, tolerance, and inhibition as alternative mechanisms of succession and identifies ways to distinguish them.

59Epidemics: Transmission produces a population threshold

An infection can disappear after a few transmissions or spread through a substantial fraction of a population. The outcome depends on susceptible hosts, contact patterns, and infectiousness. A simple population model shows how these ingredients generate a threshold that no isolated infected individual possesses.

In the susceptible-infectious-removed model, SSS, III, and RRR count individuals who can acquire infection, can transmit it, and no longer participate in transmission. The deterministic threshold describes average growth, while early transmissions in a finite population are random. Even with R0>1R_0>1R0​>1, an introduction can die out. In a population divided into interacting groups, a next-generation matrix records the expected secondary infections between groups; its dominant eigenvalue determines the invasion threshold. Thus reproduction number, outbreak probability, and growth speed are related but distinct quantities, each requiring the relevant contact and timing assumptions.

60Microbiome mutualism: Resource exchange creates community capabilities

Microbial communities contain organisms with different metabolic capabilities. Metabolism is the set of chemical transformations supporting growth and maintenance. In cross-feeding, material released by one organism becomes a resource for another. A community can therefore process substances, or sustain members, that would not be supported by the same organisms in isolation.

Suppose bacterium AAA breaks a large food molecule into fragments that bacterium BBB absorbs. This establishes a flow of material from AAA to BBB, but a benefit to BBB alone does not establish mutualism, a relationship benefiting both partners. The distinction requires comparisons of growth or persistence with and without the partner, together with interventions on the proposed exchange.

Rakoff-Nahoum and colleagues studied this distinction in gut bacteria. Bacteroides ovatus can break down inulin, a dietary chain of sugar units, outside the cell. The released products support Bacteroides vulgatus. Disabling the relevant digestive enzymes and comparing cultures and defined communities in mice showed that the producer gained a competitive benefit from the partner's presence under the tested conditions. The exact mechanism of the return benefit was not fully resolved. Other digestive systems in the same study largely retained their benefits within the producing cells.

These interactions depend on resource supply, diffusion, uptake, and competition. A partner's effect can change with the available food or with the other species present. Consequently, a community's rate of resource processing must be measured under specified conditions. A species list or a correlation between abundances cannot determine the underlying exchange: both organisms might simply respond to the same diet. The collective capability follows from the causal network of transformations and transfers.

References
  • Rakoff-Nahoum, Foster & Comstock (2016), The evolution of cooperation within the gut microbiota. Uses enzyme deletions, cultures, and communities in mice to test cross-feeding and reciprocal competitive benefits between gut bacteria.
