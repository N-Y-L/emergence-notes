Brains: collective dynamics and the limits of explanation

Neurons are cells specialized for electrical and chemical signaling. Their interactions support activity on many scales, from a voltage pulse along one cell to patterns distributed across a network. This chapter follows those scales through rhythms, sensory representation, memory, decisions, learning, and navigation.

Some mechanisms, such as the ionic currents underlying an action potential, have a detailed physical account. Others are best understood through models that show how a computation could occur. Recordings and interventions connect those models to biology: extracting a variable from neural activity shows that the activity contains information about it, while changing the activity tests its contribution to behavior.

The final entries examine two open problems. Neural criticality asks whether collective activity lies near a particular dynamical transition. Consciousness asks how neural organization relates to subjective experience. In both cases, progress depends on specifying which observations a proposed explanation predicts and how competing explanations differ.

61Action potentials: Ion channels generate a traveling electrical pulse

A neuron is a cell specialized for electrical and chemical signaling. Its membrane separates fluids containing ions, electrically charged atoms or molecules. Membrane proteins called ion channels selectively allow these ions to pass. An action potential is a brief change in membrane voltage that regenerates as it travels, allowing a signal to propagate much farther than a passive pulse on a leaky membrane.

In the classic sodium-potassium mechanism, making the cell interior less negative opens sodium channels. Sodium entry raises the voltage further and opens more channels: positive feedback. Sodium-channel inactivation and delayed opening of potassium channels end the rise and repolarize the membrane. The ensuing refractory period, during which another spike is harder or impossible to initiate, separates successive events. Hodgkin and Huxley connected these processes quantitatively to measurements on the squid's large axon, the long cell extension along which signals travel.

The propagating object is a coordinated voltage wave involving many channels. It does not require the same ions to travel the length of the axon. Chemical-energy-consuming pumps maintain the ionic concentration differences over longer times, while channel gating drives the rapid spike. Channel types and cell geometry determine the resulting wave shape, speed, and reliability.

  • Gerstner et al. (2014), Neuronal Dynamics, section 2.2: Hodgkin–Huxley Model. Derives capacitive membrane dynamics and explains sodium and potassium gating, propagation prerequisites, and refractory behavior.

62Neural oscillations: Excitation and inhibition generate a network rhythm

A population of neurons can oscillate even when its individual cells do not behave as autonomous clocks. Neurons communicate through synapses, connections that change the receiving cell's electrical state. Excitatory input promotes firing, while inhibitory input suppresses it in the operating regime considered here. Their interaction can create a repeating cycle of activation and suppression.

Let EEE and III be average activities of excitatory and inhibitory populations. Excitation recruits inhibition; inhibition reduces excitation; reduced excitation then allows inhibition to fall. Suitable response times and gains make this cycle self-sustaining.

Wilson and Cowan showed how population models can support steady states, switching, and stable limit cycles—periodic trajectories that attract nearby states. The equations here simplify that approach. Oscillatory eigenvalues near a steady state identify a possible rhythm and its damping or growth, but the nonlinear dynamics determines whether an attracting cycle exists.

A measured oscillation may instead be a damped response repeatedly excited by input, or a response to periodic external forcing. Its persistence after a drive is removed helps distinguish these cases. Population rhythms also need not require every cell to fire on every cycle: changes in the fraction of active neurons can generate a strong bulk signal. Connecting a rhythm to behavior requires a further step, such as changing its timing or coupling and measuring the resulting performance. Frequency alone does not specify what a network computes.

References
  • Wilson & Cowan (1972), Excitatory and inhibitory interactions in localized populations of model neurons. Demonstrates steady states, multiple stable states, and limit cycles in interacting excitatory and inhibitory populations.
  • Gerstner et al. (2014), Neuronal Dynamics, section 20.2: Oscillations: good or bad?. Explains network oscillations, their relation to single-cell firing, and the stability of asynchronous population activity.

63Population coding: A variable is represented across many neurons

A neuron's firing rate is the number of electrical spikes it produces per unit time. A tuning curve describes how its average rate changes with a stimulus or behavior. A cell might fire most strongly during a northeastward reach while also responding to nearby directions. Its rate alone is therefore ambiguous. A population code uses the joint activity of many neurons to represent the variable more precisely.

Suppose neuron iii has preferred direction ui\mathbf u_iui​, a unit vector, and measured firing rate rir_iri​. A population-vector decoder forms p=∑i(ri−ri,0)ui\mathbf p=\sum_i(r_i-r_{i,0})\mathbf u_ip=∑i​(ri​−ri,0​)ui​, with baseline rates ri,0r_{i,0}ri,0​. For uniformly distributed preferences and matching cosine-like tuning, components transverse to the movement cancel and the vector points along it. Georgopoulos and colleagues found that population vectors tracked reach direction in recordings from primate motor cortex, a brain region involved in movement.

Combining responses can also reduce uncertainty. Independent fluctuations average down as more informative neurons are included. Shared fluctuations behave differently: if they change the population pattern in the same way as a change in the stimulus, no decoder can reliably tell the two apart. The pattern of correlations relative to the tuning curves matters more than whether correlations are simply positive or negative.

A decoder is an explicit rule for extracting information from activity. Its success establishes that the variable is recoverable under the tested conditions. To explain the brain's computation, we must also determine how other neurons use that information and how perturbing the population affects behavior. The useful collective description is therefore the geometry and variability of the joint response, with respect to a specified stimulus or task.

References
  • Georgopoulos, Kettner & Schwartz (1988), Primate motor cortex and free arm movements to visual targets in three-dimensional space. II. Coding of the direction of movement by a neuronal population. Tests population-vector estimates of three-dimensional reach direction using directionally tuned motor-cortex neurons.
  • Moreno-Bote et al. (2014), Information-limiting correlations. Shows how noise correlations that resemble changes in the encoded variable can limit information in a neural population.

64Attractor memory: Network dynamics completes a stored pattern

Content-addressable memory retrieves a pattern from a similar or incomplete cue, rather than from a numerical storage address. A dynamical implementation makes each stored pattern an attractor: nearby network states evolve toward it. Errors in the cue can then be corrected through interactions among the units.

Each attractor has a basin: the starting states that converge to it. A damaged cue is corrected only while it remains in the appropriate basin. Too many or strongly correlated stored patterns cause interference, and unintended fixed points can appear. Simultaneous updates or asymmetric weights also remove the stated energy-descent guarantee. This model makes a specific memory mechanism understandable: distributed connections create robust retrieval regions, and retrieval errors arise when the cue or the stored associations send the dynamics into the wrong region.

References
  • Hopfield (1982), Neural networks and physical systems with emergent collective computational abilities. Introduces distributed pattern storage and retrieval through collective dynamics, with convergence under the specified symmetric asynchronous update rule.
  • Gerstner et al. (2014), Neuronal Dynamics, section 17.2: Hopfield Model. Derives energy descent and explains retrieval, storage limits, and unintended fixed points in Hopfield networks.

65Perceptual inference: Combining uncertain sensory estimates

Vision and touch can each give an uncertain estimate of an object's height. Combining them can improve precision, but the more reliable cue should carry greater weight. This is a concrete example of perceptual inference: using sensory measurements to estimate the external conditions that produced them.

Let xvx_vxv​ and xhx_hxh​ be independent, unbiased Gaussian measurements of a height xxx, with variances σv2\sigma_v^2σv2​ and σh2\sigma_h^2σh2​. Maximizing the joint likelihood gives x^=(xv/σv2+xh/σh2)/(1/σv2+1/σh2)\hat x=(x_v/\sigma_v^2+x_h/\sigma_h^2)/(1/\sigma_v^2+1/\sigma_h^2)x^=(xv​/σv2​+xh​/σh2​)/(1/σv2​+1/σh2​). Each weight is proportional to reliability, defined here as inverse variance. The combined estimate has variance (1/σv2+1/σh2)−1(1/\sigma_v^2+1/\sigma_h^2)^{-1}(1/σv2​+1/σh2​)−1. Equal uncertainties give an equal average; if one cue becomes very noisy, its weight tends to zero.

Ernst and Banks measured visual and touch judgments of height, then used the separate uncertainties to predict their combination. Human judgments closely followed the prediction under the tested conditions. Changing visual uncertainty also changed its influence, linking the weighting rule to a controlled manipulation rather than just fitting one combined estimate.

The assumptions explain when the rule should change. Correlated errors reduce the amount of independent evidence, and cues from different objects should not automatically be fused. Prior knowledge can affect the estimate when some heights were more plausible before the measurements. These are computational questions about how evidence is combined. The neural implementation is a separate question: several circuits could approximate the same weighting rule. The experiment demonstrates a reliable population-level relation between sensory uncertainty and judgment, while leaving the responsible circuit operations to be identified.

References
  • Ernst & Banks (2002), Humans integrate visual and haptic information in a statistically optimal fashion. Measures visual and touch uncertainty separately and tests the predicted reliability-weighted combination in height judgments.

66Evidence accumulation and discrete choices

In a moving-dot display, individual observations may give only weak evidence for leftward or rightward motion. Combining observations over time can improve a judgment. Evidence-accumulation models describe this process with a decision variable that changes until it reaches a stopping boundary, converting a fluctuating input stream into one of two choices.

A drift-diffusion model writes dx=v dt+σ dWtdx=v\,dt+\sigma\,dW_tdx=vdt+σdWt​, where xxx is accumulated evidence, vvv its mean drift, σ\sigmaσ the noise amplitude, and WtW_tWt​ a Wiener process with increment variance dtdtdt. Starting at zero between symmetric bounds, a larger boundary separation increases the probability of choosing the drift-favored direction and increases the mean decision time. This produces a speed-accuracy tradeoff. Observed reaction time also includes sensory and movement delays.

Shadlen and Newsome recorded activity related to motion judgments in the lateral intraparietal area, a region of monkey cortex. Some responses resembled an evolving accumulation signal. Later, Katz and colleagues reversibly inactivated this area and a sensory motion area during related tasks. Inactivation of the sensory area impaired performance, whereas inactivation of the strongly decision-correlated area had no measurable effect on the tested decisions. The correlation therefore did not establish that this area was necessary for the computation under those conditions.

The distinction matters at both the population and single-trial levels. A gradual ramp averaged over trials could reflect gradual accumulation on each trial, or abrupt changes occurring at different times. Models should therefore explain choice probabilities, response-time distributions, and suitably resolved activity. The reduction to a decision variable is useful when it predicts these observables; locating its neural implementation additionally requires causal tests.

References
  • Shadlen & Newsome (2001), Neural Basis of a Perceptual Decision in the Parietal Cortex (Area LIP) of the Rhesus Monkey. Records parietal neuronal responses associated with motion judgments and examines their relation to evidence accumulation.
  • Katz et al. (2016), Dissociated functional significance of decision-related activity in the primate dorsal stream. Compares recordings and reversible inactivation in sensory motion and parietal areas, finding different causal effects despite strong decision correlations.

67Synaptic learning: Activity changes the connections that shape future activity

A synapse is a connection through which a sending neuron influences a receiving neuron. Synaptic plasticity is a lasting change in that connection's efficacy. It provides one route by which experience changes future responses: activity modifies connections, and those modified connections alter the activity produced by later inputs.

Spike timing can determine the direction of modification. In Bi and Poo's experiments on cultured rat neurons, repeated pairs of electrical spikes strengthened certain synapses when the sending cell fired shortly before the receiving cell. Reversing the order favored weakening. The result also depended on the receiving cell type and the initial connection strength.

Inputs that repeatedly precede a receiving cell's activity can be reinforced. The rule is sensitive to temporal relationships, however caused; it cannot by itself distinguish direct influence from two cells responding to a common input.

Fast activity and slower connection changes form a coupled dynamical system. Depending on the circuit, this interaction can develop selectivity, strengthen associations, or organize attractors that support memory. Unbounded reinforcement can also produce excessive excitation, so other mechanisms must regulate connection strength or overall activity. A measured plasticity rule supplies a local mechanism; explaining a learned behavior requires showing how its accumulated changes reorganize the relevant network responses.

68Cognitive maps: Population activity represents spatial position

An animal can return to a place despite changes in its view or direction of travel. A cognitive map is an internal representation of spatial relations that can support such navigation. Neural recordings make one part of this idea concrete: patterns of activity vary systematically with location, allowing position to be inferred from them.

Place cells are neurons active around particular locations in an environment. Grid cells have multiple active locations arranged approximately as a triangular lattice. Hafting and colleagues found grid cells in the entorhinal cortex, a region connected with the hippocampus, which is involved in spatial behavior and memory. Nearby grid cells shared spacing and orientation but differed in spatial phase, the offset of their firing lattices.

Periodicity creates an ambiguity. Cells with different phases but the same lattice can distinguish positions within one repeating cell of the lattice; they cannot distinguish positions separated by a common lattice vector. Combining different spacings can extend the unambiguous range. The relevant representation is therefore distributed across cells and scales.

Navigation also requires updating position from movement. Ideal path integration is x(t)=x(0)+∫0tv(s) ds\mathbf x(t)=\mathbf x(0)+\int_0^t\mathbf v(s)\,dsx(t)=x(0)+∫0t​v(s)ds, with position x\mathbf xx and velocity v\mathbf vv. Errors accumulate, so landmarks can correct the estimate. In a continuous-attractor model, a localized activity pattern moves through a continuous family of stable population states as position changes. This supplies a possible mechanism for updating the representation. Spatial firing patterns, their response to landmark removal, and the dynamics of the full population provide different tests of that mechanism. Representing location and computing its update are related but distinct achievements.

References
  • Hafting et al. (2005), Microstructure of a spatial map in the entorhinal cortex. Documents triangular spatial firing patterns, shared grid spacing and orientation, differing phases, and relationships to landmarks.
  • O’Keefe & Dostrovsky (1971), The hippocampus as a spatial map. Preliminary evidence from unit activity in the freely-moving rat. Reports location-selective hippocampal activity during movement in rats.

69Brain criticality: Neural cascades and the criticality hypothesis

Some neural recordings contain cascades with many small events and a few very large ones. A neuronal avalanche is a cluster of detected activity events separated from other clusters by a specified period of inactivity. Its size distribution depends on recording coverage, detection thresholds, and time bins. A broad distribution motivates a criticality hypothesis, but does not alone identify a critical state.

A branching process provides a useful comparison. Each active event produces, on average, mmm descendants. For m<1m<1m<1, the expected activity decays; for m>1m>1m>1, an unbounded model allows a nonzero probability of continued growth. The boundary is m=1m=1m=1. With a nondegenerate offspring distribution of finite variance, critical branching produces a power-law tail in total cascade size. This connects broad cascades to a balance between fading and amplification.

Beggs and Plenz reported avalanche statistics in rat cortical tissue that helped motivate this interpretation. Neural networks also contain inhibition, recurrent connections, continuing input, and finite populations, so their events need not form an independent branching process. Touboul and Destexhe constructed noncritical dynamics with power-law statistics and scaling signatures, demonstrating that these observations are insufficient to identify criticality.

A stronger test combines statistics with a proposed control parameter and a response to perturbation. Changing the parameter should alter propagation in the predicted way, and scaling should remain consistent as the sampled system size changes. A claim that the network regulates itself toward the transition also needs a mechanism for that regulation. The scientific target is a specified collective propagation regime. Whether that regime improves a particular computation is another prediction to test, with both the computation and the comparison state defined.

References
  • Beggs & Plenz (2003), Neuronal avalanches in neocortical circuits. Reports avalanche-size statistics and branching estimates in rat neocortical preparations.
  • Touboul & Destexhe (2017), Power-law statistics and universal scaling in the absence of criticality. Constructs noncritical network and stochastic models with power-law avalanche statistics and scaling signatures.

70Consciousness and neural organization

Consciousness, in the sense used here, is subjective experience: what it is like to see a color, hear a sound, or feel pain. Its contents must be distinguished from wakefulness, task performance, and the ability to report. These provide evidence about experience, but are not interchangeable with it. An account of consciousness must explain which neural processes support experience and why different processes accompany different contents.

Experiments can compare neural responses across states and test the effects of interventions. Casali and colleagues used magnetic stimulation to perturb the cortex, the brain's outer sheet of neural tissue, and measured the complexity of the resulting electrical response. Their perturbational complexity index distinguished the studied conditions across wakefulness, sleep, anesthesia, and brain injury. This provides an empirically useful relation between neural response and conscious state within those conditions.

Competing theories make different organizational proposals. Global neuronal workspace theory emphasizes information becoming widely available to other brain systems. Integrated information theory relates experience to internal causal organization that cannot be reduced to independent parts. A 2025 preregistered comparison tested predictions from both theories about conscious visual content. The results supported some predictions and challenged others. Because the predictions and interpretation criteria were specified in advance, disagreements could be tied to particular observations rather than adjusted entirely after the result.

The explanatory difficulty is connecting measurable processes to experience itself. A signal associated with a report may also reflect attention, memory, or preparation to act. A proposed mechanism must distinguish these contributions and state which changes should alter experience. Emergence names the hoped-for relation between neural organization and experience; the substantive work lies in deriving discriminating predictions and testing them. A generally accepted account of that relation remains open.

References
  • Casali et al. (2013), A theoretically based index of consciousness independent of sensory processing and behavior. Tests a complexity measure of cortical responses to magnetic stimulation across specified human conscious states and clinical conditions.
  • Cogitate Consortium et al. (2025), Adversarial testing of global neuronal workspace and integrated information theories of consciousness. Compares preregistered predictions from two consciousness theories using several neural measurement methods, supporting some predictions and challenging others.
