Living matter: structures that maintain and reproduce themselves

Living systems combine several kinds of persistence. A membrane retains its shape while its molecules move; a gene circuit maintains a concentration pattern while proteins are replaced; a reproducing group passes its organization to descendants. Each calls for a different explanation. We begin with molecular structures and then add feedback, chemical energy, and reproduction.

The first examples connect familiar statistical mechanics to biology: membrane assembly, polymer elasticity, and protein folding. Reaction networks then introduce amplification, memory, and timing. The final entries examine how chemical signals specify position, how cells compensate for disturbances, and how groups of cells acquire a shared life cycle. Throughout, the useful large-scale variables are things we can measure—curvature, extension, concentration, period, or reproductive output. Explaining their behavior gives us concrete pieces of biological organization without requiring a single mechanism to explain life as a whole.

41Molecular self-assembly: Membranes from molecular interactions

Many membrane lipids have a head group that interacts favorably with water and hydrocarbon tails whose exposure to water is unfavorable. Such molecules are called amphiphiles. In water they can aggregate with their tails sheltered inside: a micelle has a small hydrophobic interior, a bilayer contains two opposing molecular layers, and a vesicle is a closed bilayer surrounding water. Simple lipid mixtures form these structures without cellular machinery.

Assembly depends on the free energy of the entire mixture. Sheltering the tails competes with the loss of translational entropy as freely dispersed molecules enter aggregates. Head groups need space, and connected tails must fill the interior without excessive stretching. These packing constraints help determine whether an aggregate favors strong curvature or an almost flat surface. The theory of Israelachvili, Mitchell, and Ninham connects molecular geometry and interaction free energies to this choice of structure.

At equilibrium, molecules exchange between aggregates and solution until their chemical potentials agree. Over an aggregation range, adding amphiphile mainly increases the amount in aggregates, while the free-molecule concentration changes much less. Molecular species, temperature, salt, and concentration determine which structures are stable. A bilayer can close into a vesicle because removing an exposed edge can outweigh the bending cost of closure.

Once a membrane exists, we can describe its bending stiffness, surface tension, and permeability without tracking every lipid. These properties belong to the extended assembly. Cellular membranes use this physical foundation, but also contain proteins and actively maintain differences between their two sides. Equilibrium assembly explains how a boundary can form; transport and chemical reactions explain how a living cell maintains and uses it.

References
  • Israelachvili, Mitchell & Ninham (1976), Theory of self-assembly of hydrocarbon amphiphiles into micelles and bilayers. Connects aggregate free energies and molecular packing constraints to the formation of micelles and bilayers.
  • Alberts et al. (2002), The Chemical Components of a Cell. Explains amphiphilic cell components and the noncovalent interactions underlying molecular assembly.

42Polymer elasticity: A molecular chain becomes an entropic spring

A polymer is a molecule made from many connected units. A long chain can resist extension even when its chemical bonds barely stretch: there are many more coiled configurations than extended ones. Holding its ends far apart reduces the number of accessible configurations and therefore raises its free energy. DNA, which stores hereditary sequence information, provides a measurable example of this entropic elasticity.

Replace the molecule by NNN independently oriented segments of length bbb, where bbb is an effective segment length. The vector constraint matters: the probability density of the distance alone also includes the spherical measure 4πR24\pi R^24πR2.

Real DNA retains orientational correlations over a persistence length. The wormlike-chain model represents this local stiffness by a bending-energy cost. Its use in DNA stretching studies explains why an ideal random walk works only after averaging over enough contour length. Close to full extension, the Gaussian spring law fails because the finite contour length becomes important; at still larger forces, stretching the molecular backbone also matters.

The collective behavior here occurs within one molecule, among its many internal degrees of freedom. Its effective spring constant measures the configurations removed by an imposed deformation. This also explains the factor of TTT: for a fixed ideal-chain geometry, heating strengthens the entropic restoring force.

  • Marko & Siggia (1995), Stretching DNA. Develops the wormlike-chain description of DNA stretching, including bending stiffness and thermal fluctuations.

43Protein folding: A sequence forms a reproducible structure

A protein is a chain of amino acids, molecular building blocks with chemically different side groups. The sequence fixes the order of these groups, while their interactions with one another and with the surrounding water favor some chain shapes over others. Many proteins repeatedly occupy a relatively narrow family of three-dimensional structures. Those structures arrange binding surfaces or bring chemical groups together to accelerate reactions, a process called catalysis.

A conformation is one spatial arrangement of the chain. We can describe folding through a free-energy landscape over conformations: nonpolar groups tend to become buried, and hydrogen bonds and other interactions favor particular local arrangements. The native state is the structural ensemble associated with the folded protein under specified conditions. Thermal motion continues within it. Refolding experiments showed that some isolated proteins recover their active structure in a suitable solution, demonstrating that the sequence and environment can be sufficient to specify this ensemble.

In a two-state approximation, collect conformations into folded and unfolded ensembles with free energies GFG_FGF​ and GUG_UGU​. Their equilibrium probabilities obey PF/PU=exp⁡[−(GF−GU)/(kBT)]P_F/P_U=\exp[-(G_F-G_U)/(k_BT)]PF​/PU​=exp[−(GF​−GU​)/(kB​T)]. A favorable folded free energy determines occupancy, while barriers on the landscape determine how quickly folding occurs. A chain can instead become trapped or aggregate with other chains. Molecular chaperones are proteins that assist folding or prevent aggregation; many consume ATP, a molecule whose chemical conversion supplies free energy.

The reduction to a few structural states makes binding and mechanical response tractable despite the enormous configuration space. It works differently for different proteins: some remain broadly disordered, and others acquire structure when they bind a partner. A structure prediction addresses which configurations are favored; explaining folding also requires the routes, rates, and cellular conditions that make them accessible.

References
  • Alberts et al. (2002), The Shape and Structure of Proteins. Explains protein sequence, stabilizing interactions, structural ensembles, and the role of molecular chaperones.
  • Cooper (2000), Protein Folding and Processing. Describes spontaneous refolding in solution and folding assistance within cells.
  • Weinreb et al. (1996), NACP, a protein implicated in Alzheimer’s disease and learning, is natively unfolded. Reports conformational measurements showing that the studied protein occupies a disordered ensemble under the tested solution conditions.

44Autocatalysis: Chemistry that accelerates its own production

A catalyst accelerates a reaction without being consumed overall in that reaction. When a product catalyzes further production of itself, its concentration affects its own growth rate: this is autocatalysis. The feedback can also run through several species, each helping produce another. Catalysis changes reaction rates; the free energy and material needed for net production still come from the supplied reactants.

The schematic reaction A+X→2XA+X\rightarrow2XA+X→2X captures the simplest amplification. Let aaa and xxx be the concentrations of supplied material AAA and catalyst XXX. With aaa fixed, a reaction-rate constant kkk, and a first-order loss rate ddd, mass-action kinetics gives x˙=(ka−d)x\dot x=(ka-d)xx˙=(ka−d)x. A small seed grows exponentially when ka>dka>dka>d and decays when losses dominate. In a closed vessel, consumption of AAA eventually removes the fixed-supply approximation. Continuous growth requires replenishment.

Lincoln and Joyce realized cross-catalytic replication with RNA. RNA is a chain molecule whose sequence can carry information and whose folded structure can catalyze reactions. Two RNA catalysts each joined supplied fragments to assemble copies of the other. Transfers into fresh substrate sustained amplification. This connects molecular reaction kinetics directly to the growth and competition of replicating populations.

A viable network must do more than contain a closed loop of reaction arrows. Production must outpace dilution and degradation, and side reactions must not consume essential intermediates too quickly. Hereditary evolution introduces a further requirement: differences between replicators must be transmitted and affect subsequent reproduction. Autocatalysis supplies amplification; the fidelity, resource supply, and physical organization of a particular system determine what can persist and evolve.

References
  • Lincoln & Joyce (2009), Self-sustained Replication of an RNA Enzyme. Demonstrates two RNA catalysts assembling copies of one another from supplied fragments, with continued amplification through substrate replenishment.
  • Hordijk & Steel (2012), Autocatalytic Sets Extended: Dynamics, Inhibition, and a Generalization. Analyzes molecular-flow dynamics and inhibition in networks satisfying autocatalytic-set criteria.

45Homochirality: Amplification of molecular handedness

A chiral molecule cannot be superimposed on its mirror image by rotation. Its two mirror forms are called enantiomers. Proteins predominantly contain amino acids of one handedness, and the sugars in DNA and RNA also follow a consistent handedness preference. Chemistry can amplify an initial imbalance between mirror forms, but which processes established biological handedness remains a historical question.

Let LLL and DDD denote the concentrations of two enantiomers. Their enantiomeric excess is e=(L−D)/(L+D)e=(L-D)/(L+D)e=(L−D)/(L+D) for nonzero total concentration. Equal amounts give e=0e=0e=0; one pure form gives ∣e∣=1|e|=1∣e∣=1. Mirror-symmetric reaction equations can have an unstable symmetric solution and stable asymmetric ones. A fluctuation can then grow even though the equations favor neither sign. A small external bias can favor one branch.

One route combines autocatalysis—each form promotes its own production—with inhibition between opposite forms. Frank's reaction scheme made this mechanism explicit. The Soai reaction subsequently demonstrated amplification of a small enantiomeric excess in a laboratory chemical system. Whether an imbalance grows depends on the actual kinetics and catalyst interactions; autocatalysis by itself does not guarantee it.

Local symmetry breaking and a shared biological preference are different scales of the problem. Independently reacting compartments could choose opposite signs, so mixing their contents could reduce the net excess. A persistent preference must survive reproduction and exchange of material, and the handedness of interacting molecular families must remain compatible. Experiments establish that nonlinear chemistry can strongly amplify asymmetry. Connecting such a mechanism to the particular molecules and environments of early life requires additional evidence.

References
  • Soai et al. (1995), Asymmetric autocatalysis and amplification of enantiomeric excess of a chiral molecule. Demonstrates amplification of an initial enantiomeric excess in an asymmetric autocatalytic reaction.
  • Frank (1953), On spontaneous asymmetric synthesis. Introduces a reaction scheme in which autocatalysis and mutual inhibition destabilize a symmetric mixture.

46Gene switches: Chemical feedback stores a cell state

A gene is a stretch of DNA used to produce a functional RNA or protein. Gene expression is the production of that molecule. Regulatory proteins can change expression by binding to DNA or to other components of the production machinery. Consequently, cells with the same DNA sequence can maintain different concentrations of gene products and behave differently.

Consider two proteins that repress each other's production. More AAA reduces BBB; less BBB releases AAA from repression. This double negative creates positive feedback. The circuit can maintain either high AAA and low BBB, or the reverse. Gardner, Cantor, and Collins built this arrangement in the bacterium Escherichia coli and used transient inputs to switch between persistent expression states.

A symmetric model is a˙=α/(1+bn)−a\dot a=\alpha/(1+b^n)-aa˙=α/(1+bn)−a and b˙=α/(1+an)−b\dot b=\alpha/(1+a^n)-bb˙=α/(1+an)−b. Concentrations a,ba,ba,b are measured relative to repression thresholds, and time in protein-loss times. The parameter α\alphaα sets maximum production and nnn the steepness of repression. Steady states occur where the two nullclines—the curves on which the respective derivatives vanish—intersect. Strong, steep repression can produce two stable asymmetric states separated by an unstable state. A perturbation that crosses the boundary between their basins changes the stored state.

Memory resides in the maintained concentration pattern. Individual proteins can be destroyed and replaced while the state persists, provided production compensates for losses and dilution as the cell grows. Molecular fluctuations can occasionally switch a small circuit, so the state's lifetime matters as well as its existence. Two reproducible long-lived states and their switching responses distinguish bistable memory from a single state that simply takes a long time to relax.

References
  • Gardner, Cantor & Collins (2000), Construction of a genetic toggle switch in Escherichia coli. Demonstrates an engineered two-repressor switch in E. coli and models the conditions supporting bistability.

47Biological clocks: A daily rhythm from molecular reactions

A circadian clock is an internal oscillator with a period close to twenty-four hours. It continues to cycle under constant environmental conditions; daily light changes can adjust its phase, or position within the cycle. The period must therefore arise from internal dynamics, even when the environment helps keep the clock aligned with day and night.

In a particularly simple reconstruction, Nakajima and colleagues combined three proteins—KaiA, KaiB, and KaiC—with ATP. These proteins belong to cyanobacteria, bacteria that obtain energy from light. The mixture sustained daily oscillations in KaiC phosphorylation, the attachment of phosphate groups to that protein, outside a cell. Its period varied comparatively little with temperature over the tested range. ATP provided the chemical free energy needed to maintain the reaction cycle.

Different chemical states of KaiC interact differently with its partners. An ordered sequence of changes at two phosphorylation sites, together with delayed inhibitory interactions, allows activity to increase, suppress itself, and recover. This illustrates why internal state matters. A single autonomous variable obeying x˙=f(x)\dot x=f(x)x˙=f(x) cannot execute a nonconstant periodic orbit: once it returns to the same value, the same derivative applies. Several chemical states, or an effective delay, allow the system to return by a different route.

Bulk oscillation also requires coordination. If molecules cycle independently with fluctuating periods, their phases spread and the population average loses its rhythm. Shared binding partners and interactions can coordinate the cycles. Thus a biochemical clock involves both a molecular sequence of events and a population mechanism that keeps their timing coherent. The Kai system isolates these problems experimentally; other organisms use different molecular circuits to generate and regulate their daily rhythms.

References
  • Nakajima et al. (2005), Reconstitution of circadian oscillation of cyanobacterial KaiC phosphorylation in vitro. Reconstitutes KaiC phosphorylation rhythms with KaiA, KaiB, KaiC, and ATP, and examines temperature dependence and mutant periods.
  • Nishiwaki et al. (2007), A sequential program of dual phosphorylation of KaiC as a basis for circadian rhythm in cyanobacteria. Identifies the ordered cycle of changes at two KaiC phosphorylation sites and their relation to clock regulation.

48Morphogenesis: From chemical gradients to biological form

Morphogenesis is the development of biological form through cell growth, movement, shape change, and organization. One early requirement is positional differentiation: different regions must activate different developmental programs. A morphogen is a chemical signal whose spatial distribution helps specify those responses. A concentration gradient can therefore translate physical position into differences in gene expression.

In an early fruit-fly embryo, the protein Bicoid forms a profile along the future head-to-tail direction and regulates other genes. At this stage many nuclei, the compartments containing DNA, share a common surrounding cellular material. Gregor and colleagues measured Bicoid concentrations, their fluctuations, and the response of a target gene. This made the precision of positional signaling a quantitative problem.

This illustrative model shows both how a length scale arises and why a change in source strength shifts the boundary.

The boundary also fluctuates because signal molecules arrive and bind randomly. Averaging over time or nearby regions, and interactions among regulatory genes, can improve reproducibility. Subsequent tissue mechanics determines how the chemical pattern becomes shape: cells exert forces and rearrange. Establishing a signal profile, interpreting it reliably, and mechanically realizing the resulting organization are distinct stages of the same developmental process.

49Cellular homeostasis: Feedback maintains a stable cellular output

Homeostasis is the maintenance of internal conditions through ongoing physiological processes. A stable output can coexist with rapid molecular turnover. The central dynamical question is how the output returns after a disturbance, even if the disturbance persists or reaction rates vary.

Bacterial chemotaxis provides a tractable example. Chemotaxis is movement biased by chemical conditions; bacteria change their swimming behavior when an attractant concentration changes. After a sustained change, their signaling activity can return close to its previous level, leaving the system responsive to further changes. Chemical modification of receptors, molecules that detect the attractant, supplies a slowly changing internal variable. Barkai and Leibler modeled this robust adaptation, and Yi and colleagues related it to integral feedback control.

Let yyy be the regulated output and let an internal variable zzz obey z˙=k(y0−y)\dot z=k(y_0-y)z˙=k(y0​−y), with k>0k>0k>0. The value of zzz accumulates the discrepancy between yyy and a reference value y0y_0y0​, and feeds back on the processes controlling yyy. At a stable steady state with finite zzz, the condition z˙=0\dot z=0z˙=0 requires y=y0y=y_0y=y0​. A constant disturbance changes the necessary controller state while leaving the output at the reference value. The result follows from the form of the feedback equation.

Its assumptions identify the operating limits. A controller with a bounded range cannot compensate for an arbitrarily large disturbance. Loss of the stored control variable can leave a residual error, and poorly matched response times can destabilize the feedback. Within its operating range, however, the circuit maintains a predictable response despite changes in its molecular components. In chemotaxis, the maintained quantity is a signaling activity; adaptation does not mean that every internal concentration remains fixed.

References
  • Barkai & Leibler (1997), Robustness in simple biochemical networks. Shows how a chemotaxis network can achieve adaptation that is robust to changes in model parameters.
  • Yi et al. (2000), Robust perfect adaptation in bacterial chemotaxis through integral feedback control. Connects chemotactic adaptation to integral feedback and identifies the conditions needed for exact steady-state adaptation.

50Multicellularity: A cluster acquires a reproducing life cycle

Multicellularity ranges from loose cell associations to organisms whose cells depend on one another. An important evolutionary step occurs when a group grows, produces descendant groups, and transmits traits of its organization. At that point, differences among groups can affect which group forms become more common.

Ratcliff and colleagues observed this process in yeast, single-celled fungi that reproduce by budding off daughter cells. They repeatedly transferred the rapidly settling portion of a culture. Cells evolved persistent attachment after division, producing branching clusters. Cluster size affected settling, and fragmentation produced smaller clusters capable of further growth. A cellular change thus generated a collective physical property that altered success under the imposed selection procedure.

Selection among groups requires heritable variation in group reproductive success. For example, groups might differ in the size at which they fragment; this difference might affect how many surviving descendant groups they produce; and descendants might resemble the parent in that trait. These conditions make a group trait evolve. Growth of a single cluster alone would establish none of the comparisons needed to show this.

Cellular and group reproduction need not favor the same changes. Faster division within a cluster can interfere with transport or mechanical integrity, whereas reduced reproduction by some cells can support collective maintenance. Development from closely related cells can reduce internal genetic conflict, and specialization can make cells increasingly dependent on the group. The yeast experiment captures an early route into this organization: repeated attachment, growth, and fragmentation connect a physical boundary to a heritable life cycle. More elaborate multicellular organisms build on additional mechanisms for development, coordination, and conflict control.

References
  • Ratcliff et al. (2012), Experimental evolution of multicellularity. Documents settling selection, persistent attachment after cell division, cluster reproduction, and evolved group traits in snowflake yeast.
