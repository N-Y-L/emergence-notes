Connections between examples

A fluid, a magnet, and an ecosystem need different collective variables. Comparing them is useful when we can identify an operation or a mechanism that survives the change of setting. The comparisons below follow from the examples in the chapters.

Which details survive a change of scale

In hydrodynamics, conservation of mass, momentum, and energy identifies variables whose long-wavelength disturbances cannot relax quickly. Molecular collisions redistribute these quantities but do not destroy them. The large-scale equations keep the conservation laws, while viscosity and thermal conductivity describe transport by the unresolved motions.

Near a continuous phase transition, correlations extend over distances much larger than the microscopic spacing. Repeated coarse-graining can then lead to universal behavior: different models approach the same limiting description. Away from that regime, the microscopic differences may matter again. The molecular details that scarcely affect a critical exponent can still determine a material's transition temperature.

A phonon illustrates a different change of variables. Instead of following each atom in a crystal, we describe coordinated vibrations and quantize their amplitudes. The useful new object is an excitation of the collective state. Changing the state of the material can change which excitations exist and how long they persist.

Growth, stability, and memory

Convection, chemical patterns, and gravitational clustering begin with disturbances that can grow. Linear stability analysis follows a small disturbance while its effect on the background is negligible. Once the disturbance becomes large, the background changes, and nonlinear dynamics determine whether growth saturates, oscillates, or continues. A calculation of onset therefore answers a narrower question than a calculation of the final pattern.

Feedback can preserve a state as well as amplify a disturbance. In a gene switch, the products of gene expression alter further expression. In an attractor model of memory, recurrent interactions bring nearby activity patterns toward the same stored pattern. These systems can share the mathematics of stable states while differing in what is stored, how the interactions are formed, and how the model is tested.

A useful collective description must retain enough information to predict the next change. If two systems have the same measured variables but evolve differently because of their histories, we have either omitted a relevant variable or need an equation with memory. Glasses and ecological resilience make this problem particularly visible.

What a model explains

The relocation model demonstrates that local decisions can produce extensive segregation without any individual specifying the final map. It does not measure the contribution of that mechanism to a particular city. To make that inference, we need observations that distinguish the proposed mechanism from alternatives such as prices, geography, and discriminatory institutions.

The same distinction applies to a simulated chemical pattern or a neural activity distribution. Reproducing a shape establishes that the chosen rules can generate it. Predicting a response to a changed condition provides a stronger test. Two models that match the same steady pattern may disagree about its relaxation time, fluctuations, or response to a disturbance.

These comparisons suggest concrete directions for further work: finding collective variables that remain predictive when conditions change; determining when discarded degrees of freedom leave long memory; and distinguishing mechanisms that produce similar observations. Each direction begins with an existing explanation and a precise limit of that explanation.
